Dust motes and embers for comp, rebuilt

MoteField adds floating dust and fire embers to a shot in Nuke from the lighting department's utility pass, with no 3D particle render. It shows up in the Nuke viewer as an ordinary image, with depth of field and motion blur, to plus or over onto the comp. This page tracks the rebuild.

This update · 5 November 2026

  • The Nuke node. A MoteField node whose output is the dust as an image: view it, then plus or over it onto the comp. Its knobs come from the settings table: a first tab with the shot, the four dials and the output, then Place, Look, Match and Deliver.
  • Frames appear as you work. A background renderer starts at the frame on screen and works outward, refreshing the viewer as frames arrive. Any render of the comp, in the GUI or on the farm, makes the frames it needs first. No buttons, nothing stale.
  • Look files in the node. Knobs show the sequence's look until you change them in the node; your changes survive reloading the look. Save look... shares a node's look.
  • Not yet run in Nuke itself. Tested against a stand-in for Nuke's Python, including the background renderer and a render filling the cache. The steps to try it are below.

In Nuke 15

It needs only the Python and numpy Nuke ships with.

  1. Install. Add the repository's v1 folder to NUKE_PATH. It adds Other > MoteField to the Nodes menu and the render callback, in the GUI and on the farm.
  2. Try the stand-in. Create the node, set Stand-in shot to wide and view it. The frame you're on appears in a second or two, then the frames around it.
  3. Your shot. Connect the utility pass Read to the node's utility input and put the mattes pass in Extra passes. Then Shot check, Fit box to shot, set the light's heading, and tune with the dials.
  4. Comp it. Plus the node onto the comp, or set Alpha to matte and merge over. Writes render any missing frames first.

The full guide, and what to report back, is docs/NUKE.md.

The new settings panel

Built from the settings table, as the Nuke node and the viewer will be. Four dials stay on screen, the tabs follow the order you work in, and each tab shows its basic settings until you ask for all of them. The tag on each setting says what changing it costs. Change something to see; double-click a setting's name to reset it.
